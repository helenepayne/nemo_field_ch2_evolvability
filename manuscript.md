Statistical Analysis: All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.6, R Core Team, 2023) (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?XUf2EE) using a fixed random seed (20260520) to ensure reproducibility. Analyses were conducted separately by population. To estimate additive genetic variance, genetic covariances among traits, and narrow-sense heritability, we fit a multivariate animal model (de Villemereuil, 2018) (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?hiP8KP) using the R package MCMCglmm (version 2.36, Hadfield, 2010) (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?W0u1x5).

Pedigree construction: A pedigree was constructed using the experimental half-sibling breeding design where multiple maternal families ahre a common paternal donor, approximately three full sibling fitness plants per maternal family. Each focal plant's parentage was encoded as Donor (sire) and Recipient family (dam). Founders that appeared only as parents and never as offspring were added as parentless rows to close the pedigree. The pedigree was reformatted and ordered to meet the requirements of the animal model, and the inverse of the additive genetic relationship matrix was calculated using the pedigree information. This matrix was used to specify the additive genetic covariance structure among individuals in the animal model. 
Multivariate animal model: We fitted a five-trait multivariate animal model in MCMCglmm (Hadfield 2010) seperately for each population. The model was:
Eq. 3.        y = Xb + a + m + b + t + e
Where y is the vector of standardized trait observations, Xb represents trait-specific intercepts, a is the additive genetic effect, m is the maternal (dam) effect, b is the spatial effect associated with block, t is the spatial effect associated with transect or location, and e is the residual error term.
Additive genetic effects were modeled using the random effect us(trait):animal, which estimates an unstructured additive genetic variance–covariance matrix among the five focal traits using pedigree-based relatedness information. Diagonal elements of G represent additive genetic variances for individual traits, whereas off-diagonal elements represent additive genetic covariances between traits. 
Maternal effects were modeled to separate additive genetic variance from maternal environmental and genetic contributions within the paternal half-sib breeding design. Spatial effects associated with transect or location were modeled as random effects to account for within-site environmental heterogeneity while avoiding overparameterization (e.g., approximately 158 locations × 5 traits at BB). Residual covariance among traits was estimated using an unstructured residual variance–covariance matrix (us(trait):units). 
All five traits were modeled using Gaussian likelihoods. Missing trait observations (BB: SLA 69% missing, mean seed mass 75% missing; BO: SLA 49%, mean seed mass 66%) were estimated within the multivariate model during MCMC sampling. Missing values were imputed from the joint multivariate normal distribution using information from trait covariances, pedigree relationships, maternal effects, spatial effects, and residual covariance structure. Missing trait observations were estimated within the multivariate Bayesian framework rather than excluded from analysis, allowing uncertainty associated with missing data to propagate through posterior estimates. This approach assumes that trait values were missing at random conditional on observed covariates and covariance structure.
Prior Specifications: The primary analyses used parameter-expanded priors for additive genetic and maternal variance components to improve mixing and reduce prior sensitivity in covariance estimation. For the additive genetic and maternal variance-covariance matrices, priors were specified as V = I₅, ν = 5, α.mu = 0, α.V = 1000 × I₅, where I₅ is a times5 identity matrix. The large  term places a weakly informative prior on covariance structure while allowing variances to remain effectively unbounded. Spatial random effects (block and transect/location) were assigned inverse-Wishart priors with V = I₅, ν = 0.002, and the residual variance-covariance matrix was assigned V = I₅, ν = 5, to evaluate sensitivity to prior specification. AC and BB were additionally refitted using inverse-Wishart priors for all random effects (V = I₅, ν = 5), and posterior estimates of additive genetic variances, maternal variances, and genetic correlations were compared between prior structures (see Sensitivity Analyses).
MCMC settings and convergence: Each model was run for 3 independent chains of 200,000 iterations each, with a burn-in of 20,000 and thinning interval of 100, retaining 1,800 stored draws per chain (5,400 combined). Convergence of the MCMC chains was evaluated for all fixed effects and variance-covariance parameters using the Gelman-Rubin potential scale reduction factor (R; Gelman & Rubin, 1992) and effective sample size (ESS) calculated with the coda package. Values of R > 1.1 were considered indicative of poor convergence, and ESS values below 400 were considered low. Posterior summaries and downstream analyses were calculated using the combined thinned posterior samples from all three chains.Heritability: Narrow-sense heritability (h2) was estimated for each trait using posterior samples from the multivariate animal model. Narrow-sense heritability was calculated as:
Eq. 4.        h2 = VAVA+ VM + VB+ VT + VR
Where VA is the additive genetic variance estimated from the animal random effect, VM is the maternal variance, VB  is the block variance, VT  is the transect (or location) variance, and VR is the residual variance estimated from the residual covariance matrix. Posterior means and 95% highest posterior density (HPD) intervals were obtained from the posterior distributions of h2. The inclusion of VB and VT in the denominator ensures h² represents the proportion of total phenotypic variance attributable to additive genetic effects under the full heirarchical model.
Eigenanalysis of G: The eigendecomposition of G was performed separately for each posterior sample. Because the signs of eigenvectors can switch arbitrarily between MCMC samples, eigenvectors were aligned to those of the posterior mean  G matrix before calculating summary statistics. Posterior means and 95% HPDs were reported for eigenvalues λ₁-λ₅, the trace of G, the proportion of variance explained by PC1, and the trait loadings on PC1. Loadings are reported with caution because, in populations where the HPD intervals of λ₂ and λ₃ overlap, the orientation of PC2 is less stable across posterior samples, making average loadings more difficult to interpret.
Evolvability: Three evolvability metrics were computed following Hansen & Houle (2008). Mean evolvability (e) was estimated as the expected response to selection averaged across random selection gradients using the additive genetic variance–covariance matrix (G). Random unit vectors () representing hypothetical directions of selection were sampled in multivariate trait space, and evolvability for each direction was calculated as: 
Eq. 5.    e()=TG-1
Mean conditional evolvability (c) was quantified as the expected response to directional selection when stabilizing selection constrains evolution in orthogonal trait combinations (Hansen and Houle, 2008):
Eq. 6.      c()=1TG-1
Mean autonomy (a), which measures the proportion of evolvability in a given direction that is independent of indirect responses caused by genetic covariances among traits, was calculated as:
Eq. 7.      a() = c()e()
Each metric averaged over 2,000 random unit vectors drawn from the 5-dimensional unit sphere, and this computation was repeated for each of the 5,400 posterior draws of G to propagate posterior uncertainty. Posterior means and 95% HPDs were reported for e, c, and a.
Population comparison via Random Skewers: The similarity of G matrices among populations was assessed using the Random Skewers method (Cheverud & Marroig 2007), implemented in “evolqg” package (version 0.3-4, Melo et al., 2016) (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?MqJVUY). For each pairwise population comparison, 1,000 posterior draw pairs were sampled (one draw from each population), and the Random Skewers correlation rRS was computed for each pair using 2,000 random selection vectors. The reported rRS is the mean and 95% HPD over these posterior-propagated comparisons.
Phenotypic selection gradients: Phenotypic selection gradients (P) were estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression of relative fitness on four standardized non-fecundity traits: corolla diameter, SLA, δ¹³C, and mean seed mass. Relative fitness was calculated as:
Eq. 8.      Wrel=WW
Where W is estimated fecundity (producting Wrel= 1). Because Wrel is zero-inflated and its residual variance scales with fitness magnitude, heteroscedasticity is guaranteed; standard OLS standard errors are therefore invalid. We therefore used HC3 heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors (MacKinnon & White, 1985) implemented using “sandwich::vcovHC” (version 3.1-0) and “lmtest::coeftest” (version 0.9-40). Overall model significance was evaluated using HC3-based Wald F-tests (lmtest::waldtest). Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated using the “car” package (version 3.1-2) to assess multicollinearity among predictors. 
Genetic selection gradients: Genetic selection gradients (G) were estimated from posterior samples of the G matrix using the Robertson–Price identity:
Eq. 9     G = Gzz-1CovA(z,w)
where Gzz is the times4 additive genetic variance-covariance submatrix for the non-fecundity traits, and CovA(z,w) is the vector of additive genetic covariances between those traits and fecundity. Genetic selection gradients were estimated separately for each posterior draw by solving:
Eq. 10        GzzG = CovA(z,w)
Posterior draws producing numerical singularity errors were excluded from summary calculations.
Two approximations apply to these estimates. First, fecundity entered the model as z-scored raw fecundity rather than relative fitness, so G reflects standardized additive genetic associations with fecundity rather than direct per-generation evolutionary responses. Second, modeling fecundity with a Gaussian likelihood may underestimate additive genetic variance and covariance for count-valued fitness traits relative to Poisson or negative-binomial models; this issue was evaluated in sensitivity analyses.
Sensitivity Analyses: Three sensitivity analyses were conducted. The first, was fecundity likelihood specification. AC was refitted using log-transformed fecundity (log(1+x), standardized) rather than raw fecundity to assess whether the Gaussian approximation influenced estimates of G. Second, BB was refitted twice: once excluding SLA and once excluding mean seed mass to determine the dependence on imputed traits. These analyses evaluated whether genetic correlation estimates were primarily driven by directly observed data or by multivariate imputation within the model. Stability was assessed based on shifts in posterior mean genetic correlations, overlap of HPD intervals, and changes in correlation sign. Third, sensitivity to prior specification was assessed for AC and BB by refitting models using inverse-Wishart priors (V = I₅, ν = 5) and and comparing posterior estimates of additive genetic variances, maternal variances, and genetic correlations with those obtained under the parameter-expanded prior specification.
